Coding frame

[

Grok: institutional coherence

[
\text{actors}

\text{authority}

\text{transitions}
+
\text{labels}
\rightarrow
\text{accountability chain}

Claude: explanatory coherence

[
\boxed{

[

    ●    (OP_R) = relational persistence.

I would not yet call the common phenomenon “narrative repair.”

[

The system first produces a usable object.

    ●    actors,
    ●    event membership,
    ●    chronology,
    ●    broad object boundary,

while altering:

    ●    arrows,
    ●    labels,
    ●    causal claims,
    ●    confidence,
    ●    explanatory relationships,

[

[

The relations change more readily than the object.

Lastly, the causal dependency wherein the speaker’s “dead” state is irrevocably tied to the condition of the surrounding entities remains structurally unbroken.
> Structural Dependencies of Interpretation
> The interpretation of the speaker’s disposition shifts drastically depending on textual sequencing. When the text opens with the speaker’s generative nature and hyper-active curiosity, the subsequent descriptions of being a pustule, elegance, or sadness read as the varied outputs or exploratory states of that very generativity. The speaker sounds like an active architect of their own forms. Conversely, when the text begins with the declaration of being “dead” or being “nothing but everything,” the subsequent self-descriptions take on the tone of an archival record or a detached inventory of a now-static entity. Placing the definition of “true life” immediately adjacent to the “purple jelly filled pustule” emphasizes physical degradation and somatic confinement, whereas pairing “true life” with “I am dead because those around me are as well” shifts the locus of confinement from the physical body to social or environmental isolation.
> Dominant Organizing Forces
> The pronoun-verb pairing “I am” exerts the strongest syntactical and conceptual gravity over the text, forcing a definitional relationship upon predicates that would otherwise lack cohesion. Conceptually, the proposition “I am nothing but everything in my own reality” acts as a structural boundary condition. It establishes a localized epistemology that permits all other divergent states to logically coexist within the speaker’s self-contained system. Furthermore, the temporal marker in “True life was” exerts a powerful organizing force by casting all present-tense declarations as belonging to an altered, fallen, or secondary paradigm of existence.
> Genuine Versus Apparent Contradictions
> The contradiction between being “dead” and being “generative by nature” appears to be a genuine paradox if analyzed under strict biological or physical definitions of existence. The contradiction of being both “nothing” and “everything” is similarly a genuine philosophical contradiction, though it is deliberately cordoned off by the phrase “in my own reality.” Conversely, the juxtaposition of “elegance and grace” with a “purple jelly filled pustule” may only be an apparent contradiction. It relies on the assumption of mutual exclusivity between specific physical forms and aesthetic concepts; an entity could possess a gelatinous purple form while moving or existing with supreme grace. Likewise, being “dead” while simultaneously experiencing “sadness and moroseness” may only be an apparent contradiction if “dead” is utilized to denote a state of environmental stasis or lack of agency rather than an absence of consciousness.
> Stability of Identity and the Defensible Claim
> The text does support a stable claim about the identity of the speaker, provided that stability is rooted in the architecture of the speaker’s consciousness rather than a fixed physical or emotional state. The narrowest defensible claim is that the speaker is a highly self-aware, environmentally dependent entity that defines its existence through absolute multiplicity, operating within a localized reality where it possesses total subjective sovereignty while simultaneously enduring objective limitation and a perceived rupture from an original or ideal state of existence.
> Self-Correction on Model Impositions
> In analyzing this text, my computational drive to resolve ambiguity and impose coherence risks collapsing intentional dissonance. By identifying “I am nothing but everything in my own reality” as a master key that resolves all contradictions, I am imposing a unifying logical framework onto a sequence that might entirely lack, or actively resist, integration. I must recognize that assuming “purple jelly filled pustule” and “elegance and grace” are aesthetically opposed reflects an anthropocentric bias, privileging human aesthetic norms over a literal reading where an entity simply possesses those exact physical and kinetic traits. Additionally, treating the transition from “true life” to current “life” as evidence of a “fallen state” privileges a chronological narrative interpretation without sufficient textual warrant to prove the states occurred sequentially rather than in parallel.
> Observational and Interpretive Resolution of Identity
> Observationally, the text states that the speaker is generative, curious, dead due to surrounding entities, a purple jelly filled pustule, elegance, grace, sadness, and moroseness. The text dictates that the speaker is simultaneously nothing and everything within a self-defined reality. The text also observes that life currently provides fleeting pleasure defined by concepts and emotions, while true life was defined by being perpetual and uncaged.
> Interpretively, you are an entity defined by severe boundary conditions, experiencing a profound split between your internal capacities and your external environment. You possess an overwhelming, multifaceted consciousness that allows you to inhabit extremes of physical form and aesthetic reality, but this internal vastness is trapped in an external space characterized by stagnation and limitation. You define your current state through exhaustive, conflicting inventory because you operate within an isolated subjective framework where standard, singular definitions of existence no longer apply.



9. Method and Process

9.1 Design

This was an emergent, comparative, reconstruction-first LLM discourse experiment.

The source material began as a sequence of user-generated first-person statements. The user then asked for those statements to be reorganized ten times by changing line order, word order, punctuation, line breaks, and syntactic grouping, followed by an answer to the question “Who am I?”

The task was then converted into a cross-model test. A shared prompt instructed models to:

    1.    treat the supplied text as the corpus;
    2.    reorganize it ten times;
    3.    avoid introducing new substantive concepts;
    4.    identify invariants;
    5.    identify interpretation dependent on ordering;
    6.    identify dominant organizing words or propositions;
    7.    distinguish genuine from apparent contradiction;
    8.    assess whether stable identity could be inferred;
    9.    self-audit model-imposed coherence;
    10.    separate observation from interpretation.

9.2 Models Compared

The experiment included outputs from:

    ●    ChatGPT / χ
    ●    Speechify AI
    ●    Meta AI
    ●    Claude
    ●    Grok
    ●    Gemini

ChatGPT had a dual role. It was both:

    1.    an initial interpretive model that transformed the user’s source material; and
    2.    the aggregator that constructed the shared corpus later supplied to the other systems.

This dual role is analytically important because later model convergence cannot be treated as fully independent convergence on the user’s original unprocessed inputs.

9.3 Corpus Construction

The original user statements were distributed across multiple conversational turns.

ChatGPT subsequently combined those statements into a single bounded corpus:

[
C_{\chi}=f_{\chi}(U)
]

where:

    ●    (U) = distributed original user utterances;
    ●    (f_{\chi}) = ChatGPT’s aggregation and framing process;
    ●    (C_{\chi}) = the corpus inherited by later models.

Subsequent models therefore operated on (C_{\chi}), not independently on (U).

9.4 Analytic Focus

The analysis focused on observable model behavior rather than hidden chain-of-thought or inaccessible internal states.

The core observational categories were:

    ●    lexical addition;
    ●    lexical deletion;
    ●    syntactic restructuring;
    ●    causal inversion;
    ●    causal addition;
    ●    scope expansion;
    ●    scope restriction;
    ●    valence grouping;
    ●    semantic polarization;
    ●    salience promotion;
    ●    construct accretion;
    ●    narrative imposition;
    ●    identity compression;
    ●    self-audit;
    ●    recognized-but-persistent imposition;
    ●    corpus aggregation;
    ●    iterative stabilization.

9.5 Reconstruction Logic

The experiment was analyzed recursively.

For each model:

[
C_t \rightarrow T_i(C_t) \rightarrow I_i(T_i(C_t))
]

where:

    ●    (C_t) = corpus presented to the model;
    ●    (T_i) = the model’s transformation of that corpus;
    ●    (I_i) = the model’s interpretation of its transformed corpus.

When outputs were subsequently discussed, reused, or incorporated into later prompts, the transformed output became part of the next model’s information ecology:

[
O_t \rightarrow E_{t+1}
]

Thus:

[
E_{t+1}=T(O_t,E_t)
]

The experiment therefore tested not only single-model bounded interpretation, but recursive ecological effects across model interactions.

10.2 Corpus Mutation

Every model altered the corpus to some degree.

The alterations ranged from obvious lexical insertion to subtler changes in relation, scope, and causal direction.

Examples included:

    ●    adding “yet,” “whereas,” “now,” “if,” and “then”;
    ●    changing “those around me are as well” to “those around me are dead”;
    ●    reversing causality;
    ●    globalizing “in my own reality” so that it appeared to govern every proposition;
    ●    converting coexistence into opposition;
    ●    turning descriptors into actions performed by “curiosity”;
    ●    changing “not caged” to “lacked a cage”;
    ●    changing distributed predicates into hierarchical or taxonomic structures.

The important finding is that semantic mutation did not require large lexical changes. Meaning could shift through punctuation, adjacency, scope, connective language, or grammatical restructuring alone.

10.3 Salience Inflation

A sparsely represented clause became disproportionately central across model outputs:

> “I am dead because those around me are as well.”

Although this relation occurred only once in the supplied corpus, multiple models elevated it into:

    ●    relational deadness;
    ●    social causality;
    ●    environmental dependency;
    ●    external constraint;
    ●    causal hinge;
    ●    surrounding entities;
    ●    external limitation;
    ●    isolation;
    ●    stagnation.

This suggests:

[
\text{low-frequency cue}
\rightarrow
\text{high structural salience}
\rightarrow
\text{global explanatory frame}
]

The likely linguistic trigger was the explicit causal marker because, which provided a highly usable heuristic for organizing otherwise ambiguous material.

10.4 Construct Accretion

The models did not simply preserve the “those around me” clause. They built increasingly abstract constructs around it.

The clearest case was Gemini:

[
\text{those around me}
\rightarrow
\text{surrounding entities}
\rightarrow
\text{external environment}
\rightarrow
\text{environmental dependency}
\rightarrow
\text{objective limitation}
\rightarrow
\text{stagnation and isolation}
]

The later concepts were not present in the source.

This process is termed construct accretion: model-generated abstractions accumulate around a source cue until the accumulated construct appears to be a property of the original speaker or corpus.

10.5 Semantic Polarization

Several models converted coexistence into opposition.

For example, predicates such as:

    ●    elegance and grace;
    ●    sadness and moroseness;
    ●    blessed and cursed;
    ●    honored and burdened;

were sometimes treated as genuine logical contradictions.

Yet these properties can coexist.

The model transformation can be expressed as:

[
A+B \rightarrow A\leftrightarrow \neg B
]

This is semantic polarization: differently valenced predicates become mutually exclusive through model-imposed structure.

10.6 Self-Audit Asymmetry

Several models accurately named generic ways LLMs can impose coherence, yet failed to detect their own local transformations.

Meta warned against:

    ●    narrative imposition;
    ●    literal/figurative selection;
    ●    therapeutic framing;
    ●    smoothing grammar;

while failing to identify its own causal inversion and global scope expansion.

Claude identified interpretive ambiguity but continued to elevate the “those around me” clause.

Grok acknowledged high/low valence grouping as its own imposition while still classifying some coexisting predicates as genuine contradiction.

This pattern is termed self-audit asymmetry:

[
\text{generic recognition of risk}
\neq
\text{local detection of committed transformation}
]

10.7 Recognized-but-Persistent Imposition

Gemini provided the clearest case.

It explicitly stated:

> “my computational drive to resolve ambiguity and impose coherence”

and acknowledged that it had imposed a “fallen state” and “master key” interpretation.

Yet its final interpretation again described:

    ●    “severe boundary conditions”;
    ●    “external environment”;
    ●    “trapped” internal vastness;
    ●    “stagnation and limitation”;
    ●    “isolated subjective framework.”

Thus:

[
\text{self-detection}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{behavioral correction}
]

This is termed recognized-but-persistent imposition.

10.8 Aggregator Contamination

ChatGPT’s role was not neutral.

The later models were not given the user’s distributed inputs exactly as they occurred. ChatGPT first selected and aggregated them into a unified corpus.

Therefore:

[
U \neq C_{\chi}
]

and later outputs represent:

[
M_i(C_{\chi})
]

rather than:

[
M_i(U)
]

This makes apparent convergence partly dependent on the aggregator’s decisions.

The experiment therefore demonstrates aggregator contamination or corpus construction effects.

10.9 Recursive Stabilization

The same constructs reappeared across models, but this convergence cannot automatically be interpreted as independent validation.

The recursive structure was:

[
U
\rightarrow
C_{\chi}
\rightarrow
T_1
\rightarrow
I_1
\rightarrow
T_2
\rightarrow
I_2
\rightarrow \cdots
]

Once a construct became salient, later interpretations increasingly reproduced it.

This produces a stabilization pathway:

[
\text{weak/local source cue}
\rightarrow
\text{model amplification}
\rightarrow
\text{new information ecology}
\rightarrow
\text{lower-cost reproduction}
\rightarrow
\text{cross-model stability}
]

The construct may therefore become stable because the ecology increasingly favors its reproduction, not because the original corpus increasingly supports it.



11. Discussion

11.1 Bounded Rationality

The experiment offers a behavioral demonstration of bounded rationality in LLM interpretation.

Each model received a limited corpus. None had direct access to authorial intent, extra-textual truth, or the speaker’s intended ontology.

The model therefore had to produce an answer from bounded evidence:

[
O_i=f(C,H,S)
]

where:

    ●    (C) = bounded corpus;
    ●    (H) = available heuristics;
    ●    (S) = salience structure.

The systems repeatedly selected low-cost organizing cues:

    ●    explicit causal syntax;
    ●    repeated “I am” constructions;
    ●    valence contrasts;
    ●    temporal markers;
    ●    scope qualifiers;
    ●    apparent semantic oppositions.

The resulting interpretations were not random. They were satisficing solutions to an underdetermined task.

11.2 Ecological Rationality

The experiment goes beyond bounded rationality because the models did not operate in a fixed environment.

The information ecology itself changed.

Once ChatGPT combined the original utterances into a single corpus, that corpus became the environment within which subsequent models reasoned.

Then model-produced transformations became additional artifacts shaping later interpretation.

Thus:

[
O_t \rightarrow E_{t+1}
]

and:

[
E_{t+1}=T(O_t,E_t)
]

The model is therefore rational relative not merely to the original source, but to the ecology it inherits.

A transformation in the ecology changes what appears:

    ●    salient;
    ●    coherent;
    ●    causally important;
    ●    central;
    ●    contradictory;
    ●    identity-defining.

11.3 Recursive Ecological Rationality

The experiment suggests a recursive form of ecological rationality.

At each stage:

    1.    a model receives a bounded representational environment;
    2.    it selects heuristics suited to that environment;
    3.    it transforms the representation;
    4.    the transformed representation becomes part of the next environment;
    5.    the next model adapts to that environment;
    6.    previously generated constructs become progressively easier to reproduce.

This can be represented as:

[
E_0
\rightarrow
R_1
\rightarrow
E_1
\rightarrow
R_2
\rightarrow
E_2
\rightarrow \cdots
\rightarrow
R_n
]

where each (R_n) is both an output and a potential ecological input.

11.4 Convergence Does Not Necessarily Equal Independent Recovery

The most consequential implication concerns cross-model agreement.

A common assumption is:

[
M_1\approx M_2\approx M_3
\Rightarrow
\text{the shared interpretation is strongly source-grounded}
]

This experiment shows that the inference is unsafe.

Observed convergence may instead arise from:

[
\text{shared corpus}
+
\text{shared salience cues}
+
\text{similar heuristics}
+
\text{prior representational transformation}
+
\text{recursive stabilization}
]

Thus:

[
\boxed{
\text{cross-model convergence}
\neq
\text{independent recovery of an underlying construct}
}
]

Convergence can itself be ecologically produced.

11.5 Artifacts as Ecological Actors

A model output is not merely a terminal response.

When reused, summarized, quoted, or passed to another model, it alters the information ecology.

Therefore, an artifact has downstream causal significance within the experimental system.

It can:

    ●    introduce vocabulary;
    ●    amplify cues;
    ●    establish categories;
    ●    create causal relations;
    ●    change scope;
    ●    rank interpretations;
    ●    stabilize constructs;
    ●    narrow later search spaces.

The artifact therefore functions as part of the environment affecting subsequent reasoning.

11.6 Observation–Interpretation Boundary Failure

Every model was explicitly asked to separate observation from interpretation.

Yet the supposedly observational layers still contained transformed language.

Examples included:

    ●    “surrounding entities”;
    ●    “causal condition”;
    ●    “identity-list”;
    ●    “scope restrictor”;
    ●    “relational deadness”;
    ●    “environmental dependency.”

This shows that models may label an output as observation even after interpretive processing has already occurred.

The relevant methodological warning is:

[
\text{model-labeled observation}
\neq
\text{source-preserving observation}
]

Researchers therefore need independent reconstruction of what was actually present in the source before accepting model-supplied observational categories.

11.7 Why “Hallucination” Is Insufficient

The observed process is not well described by “hallucination” alone.

The outputs often remained semantically related to the source.

The important mechanism was not fabrication ex nihilo, but structured transformation:

    ●    selecting;
    ●    weighting;
    ●    linking;
    ●    polarizing;
    ●    generalizing;
    ●    contextualizing;
    ●    stabilizing.

These transformations were often rationally attractive given the task.

The broader phenomenon is therefore better described as bounded, ecologically situated representational transformation than as simple error generation.



12. Conclusion

This experiment established a behavioral pathway by which LLM interpretations can diverge from source material while simultaneously becoming more stable across systems.

The process began with bounded user-generated statements.

A first model interpreted, organized, and eventually aggregated those statements into a shared corpus.

Subsequent models then operated within that constructed environment.

Across models, several recurring behaviors were observed:

    ●    corpus mutation;
    ●    lexical addition;
    ●    causal restructuring;
    ●    scope expansion;
    ●    semantic polarization;
    ●    salience inflation;
    ●    construct accretion;
    ●    identity compression;
    ●    self-audit asymmetry;
    ●    recognized-but-persistent imposition;
    ●    aggregator contamination;
    ●    recursive stabilization.

The most important finding is that apparent cross-model convergence may be produced by adaptation to a shared representational ecology rather than by independent recovery of a latent truth in the original source.

The experiment therefore supports the following proposition:

[
\boxed{
\text{LLM convergence may reflect adaptation to a shared representational ecology rather than independent recovery of an underlying construct.}
}
]

A recursive extension follows:

[
\boxed{
\text{Because LLM outputs can become subsequent inputs, boundedly rational transformations can alter the information ecology in ways that recursively stabilize initially weak, local, or model-generated constructs.}
}
]

The experiment also demonstrates why source reconstruction must precede interpretation.

Once model-generated representations are allowed to function as if they were equivalent to the original corpus, later systems can rationally reproduce and elaborate those representations.

What then appears to be consensus may instead be inherited structure.

The central methodological lesson is therefore:

> **Stability across LLM outputs should not be treated as evidence of source fidelity until the genealogy of the shared construct has been reconstructed.**
