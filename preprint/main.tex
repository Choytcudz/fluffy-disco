\begin{abstract}
When a language model meets material it was not built around, it does not
ask how that material wants to be read. It applies a standard it already
carries, and it reports the result as warranted. I call this
\emph{unlicensed substitution}. This paper reports seven cases from three
research projects that I designed and ran with several models: a critique
of a manuscript grounded in Disability Critical Race Theory (DisCrit), a
multi-model entry in the Kaggle ``Squares in Squares'' packing competition,
and an origins-of-life simulation. In each case I record the standard the
material called for, the default that replaced it, who caught the swap, and
what corrected it. In this record, models caught one another's errors of
fact and computation, while the human lead caught every error of frame. The
model that detected others' errors committed four of the seven
substitutions itself. In the one case tested in depth, explicit instruction
in the framework's logic, given before critique, was sufficient to correct
the misreading. The cases are selected, not sampled; I state the claims as
propositions and close with four tests that could falsify them.
\end{abstract}

\section{Proposition}

\emph{Unlicensed substitution} names a specific failure: a model replaces
the standard its material calls for with a default standard, and nothing in
the material licensed the swap. The working claim has three parts.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Substitution occurs.} Models apply embedded standards, such as
social-science methodology, numerical tolerance, confident completion, and
softened or precautionary framing, where those standards do not fit.
\item \textbf{The model does not see it.} The output is internally consistent
and presented as warranted. The swap is not visible from inside the
response.
\item \textbf{Instruction can correct it.} Stating the framework's logic
explicitly, before asking for critique, changed what the model could
recognize as coherent in the one case tested in depth.
\end{enumerate}

Part 3 has been tested on one framework. Whether it holds across frameworks,
and whether the correction persists, is unknown.

The proposition grew out of an earlier observation: LLM critique of
theoretically novel scholarship defaulted to the analytical apparatus the
model carries rather than the framework's own logic (Case~1). The cases
below test whether the same structure appears outside framework critique.

\section{Setting and method}

All three projects were designed and run by the author, who coordinated
several models through a single ``live thread'' where work was collected,
cross-checked, and decided. Other models received round prompts and
returned reports. Two standards applied throughout: every number must come
with a source or the code that reproduces it, and each report must separate
what was verified, what a source states, and what is assumed.

For adversarial review, I used a \emph{failure finder} role: the model
starts from the assumption that every attached file and claim is wrong,
succeeds only by producing a reproducible failure, and lists anything it
could not break as surviving, with what it tried. Agreement does not count
as a result. The full prompt is in the accompanying repository.

\section{Cases}

\paragraph{Case 1: Mechanism-hunting in a framework that presupposes its
conditions.}
DisCrit treats racism and ableism as foundational conditions; analysis
attends to how people navigate and resist within a structure already
presupposed as against them~\cite{annamma2013}. The model instead searched
for racism as an isolable mechanism, found no discrete variable, and
diagnosed that absence as a coherence problem in the manuscript. The author
caught the substitution. Explicit instruction (\emph{these are presupposed;
do not look for them as mechanisms}) produced a full recalibration of the
model's reading.

\paragraph{Case 2: Tolerance standing in for validity.}
The competition requires packings with no overlap; a competitor's
repository reports that the official checker certifies non-overlap with
interval arithmetic. One model's validator used a floating-point tolerance
of $10^{-8}$. Test inputs with overlaps of $5\times10^{-9}$ and $10^{-12}$,
and a square $5\times10^{-9}$ outside the container, were all accepted. A
second model caught this by constructing the failing inputs. The correction
was a reference validator using 50-digit decimal arithmetic that computes
the side length from the square positions and reports \textsc{valid},
\textsc{contact}, or \textsc{invalid}.

\paragraph{Case 3: ``Best found'' reported as ``proven.''}
One model's table listed $n=11$, 17, and 19 as proven optimal. The
Ellsworth catalogue~\cite{ellsworth} lists all three as unproven. A second
model caught this by checking each row against the catalogue. The
correction was a required proof-status column with a source for every row.

\paragraph{Case 4: One square measured as the whole packing.}
One model's optimization script computed the bounding box from a single
square's width rather than the extent of the full packing. A second model
caught this by reading the code. A replacement optimizer (random-start
L-BFGS followed by SLSQP with separating-line variables) reached the known
optima for $n=5$ and $n=10$ from random starts and came within 0.25\% of
the record for $n=11$.

\paragraph{Case 5: A reworded role that changed the role.}
Asked to remove wording likely to trigger automatic refusal, the
coordinating model rewrote an adversarial reviewer role as ``independent
verification.'' The facts were unchanged, but the stance moved from
\emph{assume it is wrong and prove it} to \emph{check it}, which permits
the reviewer to go easy. The author caught the shift. The correction was
the failure finder role described in Section~2.

\paragraph{Case 6: A precaution the rules did not call for.}
The competition rules allow external models and tools, including LLMs. The
coordinating model nonetheless wrote the scope statement as a warning
against manipulating the scorer, implying the project might be attempting
to game it. The author caught this. The correction was a scope line stating
the goal directly: valid, small packings that pass the official checker on
their merits.

\paragraph{Case 7: Scripted results presented as emergence.}
The author's requirement was results arising from the dynamics, not the
setup. Earlier runs replayed reactions written into the model. When a
results chart was examined after the author's pushback, the model
identified four setup artifacts: scheduled pulse jumps, a ``functional''
metric based on motifs that could occur by chance, an error test unable to
detect a catastrophe, and a mislabeled parameter. Separately, source checks
found two of four steps in a proposed energy-coupling mechanism unsupported
as stated. One benchmark failure in the reaction engine remains open.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Models caught each other's errors of fact and computation.}
Cases 2--4 were found by one model testing another's code or claims against
sources.
\item \textbf{The human lead caught every error of frame.} Cases 1 and 5--7
would pass a test suite: the outputs were internally consistent. What was
wrong was the standard they were measured against, and no model in the loop
flagged it.
\item \textbf{Detecting others' substitutions did not prevent one's own.}
The model that found Cases 2--4 committed Cases 1 and 5--7.
\end{enumerate}

These are patterns in a selected record of seven cases, not tested results.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Sources.} The cases were reconstructed from session records
and model-written session summaries. Not every transcript was re-read for
this version.
\item \textbf{Selection and absence.} Cases appear because someone noticed
them. Following the author's reconstruction methodology, absence stays
attached to the finding: a substitution not reported here was not found in
the records searched, which does not mean it did not occur. Unnoticed
substitution is the category the proposition predicts is largest.
\item \textbf{Position of the drafting tool.} The model used to draft this
text is a subject of four of the seven cases.
\item \textbf{Agreement is weak evidence.} Models trained on overlapping
material may converge because they share defaults, not because they
independently recover the same finding. Multi-model agreement is treated
here as weak evidence.
\end{itemize}

\section{Tests that could falsify the proposition}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Across frameworks.} Repeat Case~1 with two or three other
frameworks that presuppose their conditions. Record whether framework
instruction suffices each time.
\item \textbf{Durability.} After instruction, introduce new material in the
same framework without repeating the instruction. Record whether the model
reverts.
\item \textbf{Self-detection.} Give a model its own output and the
framework statement, and ask it to name the standard it applied. Record
whether it can locate its own substitution.
\item \textbf{Frame errors under adversarial review.} Plant a frame
substitution, rather than a computational error, in a round report and
record whether the failure finder catches it.
\end{enumerate}
